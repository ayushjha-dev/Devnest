California's governor has signed a state law that limits AI being used as an **automated boss**. In simple terms: algorithms should not make big decisions about workers on their own.

This is one of the more concrete AI regulations to come out of the US, and it is worth understanding precisely what it does — because the summary versions have been getting the detail wrong.

## What The Law Does

Some companies use software to assign tasks, track performance, or even decide who gets disciplined. This law aims to make sure **a human stays involved** in important decisions about people's jobs.

The distinction is the whole point. It is not a ban on AI in the workplace. Software can still recommend, rank, score and flag. What the law restricts is a system making the **final call on its own**, with no meaningful human in the loop.

## Why It Matters

**Workers may get more fairness and transparency.** There is a pattern in algorithmic hiring that researchers have documented for years: models trained on historical hiring data learn to reproduce historical bias, because past hiring decisions were themselves biased. Keeping a human accountable does not make the system fair, but it makes the outcome contestable.

**Companies may need to review how they use AI in management.** Most firms adopted AI HR tools quickly, often without documenting how the tools reach decisions. This law turns that habit into a legal exposure. If you cannot show a human meaningfully reviewed a decision, the decision is the vulnerable one.

**Other states often follow California's lead.** This is the practical reality of US AI regulation. California is large and litigious, and companies building for one market of that size tend to build for all of them. Expect other states to introduce variations rather than to wait.

## Questions To Watch

How strict will enforcement be, and how will businesses adapt?

Those are the two open questions, and they matter enormously. If enforcement is light, this is a press-release law. If it is serious, it becomes a template. Watch for whether companies start adding a genuine human sign-off step, or simply insert a rubber-stamp click to satisfy the letter of the requirement. The difference between those two responses is the difference between real accountability and compliance theatre.

## Where Algorithms Already Make Work Decisions

AI in the workplace is not new. It is also further along than most people realise, which matters because the law addresses decisions that are already routine.

**Screening and ranking.** Many large employers use an automated system to sort applications before a human reviews them. Rejected applications may never be seen by a person at all, which makes the system itself the decision-maker regardless of what the policy document says.

**Performance monitoring.** Software aggregates activity data — messages sent, tickets closed, keystroke patterns on some systems — into scores used in reviews.

**Scheduling and allocation.** Algorithms decide shifts, assign routes and distribute workload. These feel administrative, but they determine income for many workers.

**Performance management and discipline.** Some employers automate parts of the warning or termination process, particularly for high-volume, frontline roles.

**Pay and promotion.** Compensation models recommend raises and levels, and where recommendations are effectively decisions, the distinction becomes academic.

The common thread is that these systems are usually introduced as efficiency tools and then **quietly acquire decision authority** through default acceptance. Nobody decided to let a model fire someone. It simply became the last step in an approved process, and nobody revisited the step.

## Why "A Human Is Involved" Is Harder Than It Sounds

The law's premise is that meaningful human involvement helps. That is generally right, and it is also the reason the wording matters enormously.

There is a spectrum, and the difference between the ends is the difference between real accountability and a rubber stamp:

**Genuine review.** A person examines the recommendation, has the authority and information to override it, and does so when warranted.

**Nominal review.** A person clicks approve on a queue. The override rate is near zero because there is no time to investigate and no visibility into why the system decided as it did.

**Absent review.** The decision is automatic, and "human oversight" exists only in an organisational chart.

Research on algorithmic management consistently finds the middle category is the common one: a human technically in the loop, formally responsible for the outcome, and practically unable to examine the underlying reasoning. That person carries the liability without holding the information, which is the least fair arrangement available.

This is the single most important implementation question. If the rule merely requires a click, it gets a click.

## What Companies Should Be Doing Now

For anyone operating in California or a state likely to follow, sensible preparation does not require waiting for guidance:

**Document every decision made by a system.** Which tools rank, score, filter or recommend anything about a person. You cannot govern a decision you have not written down.

**Define the human checkpoint in advance.** Who reviews, what they see, what authority they have to override, and how long they have. Ambiguity here produces nominal review by default.

**Measure override rates.** If nobody ever overrides the system, that is strong evidence the human step is decorative. It is also the number a regulator would look at first.

**Check the training data.** Models trained on historical decisions reproduce historical patterns, including historical bias. The data is often the actual source of the problem.

**Offer a route to appeal.** A decision a person cannot contest is not meaningfully human-reviewed. This is both good practice and increasingly a legal expectation.

## The Wider Significance

California has a long history of regulating on behalf of people who lack organised political power, and this law fits that pattern. Employment decisions have always been the area where the gap between what a contract permits and what a person actually experiences is widest.

Two things are worth watching beyond California.

**Does it survive legal challenge?** Litigation under state privacy and employment statutes has become a standard tactic for delaying regulation. Expect an industry challenge, and expect the text to be narrowed by whatever the courts decide.

**Does it become a template?** Several states have signalled interest in comparable rules, and multistate employers frequently adopt the strictest applicable standard rather than maintaining separate compliance. If California holds, the practical effect will extend well beyond its borders.

## The Underlying Principle

Strip away the specifics and the law rests on one idea: **a decision that affects someone's livelihood should be attributable to a person.**

That is a modest principle, and it is not radical. It is roughly how liability worked before decisions were automated. The interesting part is that the law is having to state it explicitly, which suggests it was already being eroded by default rather than by choice.

## Takeaway

If AI helps decide who gets hired, rated or fired, people want a human to be accountable. This law is an early example of that idea becoming rules.

It is also a useful signal for anyone working in tech: the era of shipping an AI decision feature with no recourse path is ending. If you build anything that ranks, scores or filters people, building in an appeal and review step now is much cheaper than retrofitting it after a lawsuit.
